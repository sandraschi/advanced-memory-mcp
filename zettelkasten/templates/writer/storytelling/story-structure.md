# Story Structure

The architecture that holds a story together and guides reader through a satisfying journey.

## Three-Act Structure

### Act 1: Setup (25%)
**Purpose**: Establish normal world, introduce protagonist, inciting incident

**Key Beats**:
1. **Opening**: Hook reader immediately
2. **Ordinary World**: Show protagonist's normal life
3. **Inciting Incident**: Event that disrupts normal (10-15% in)
4. **First Plot Point**: Protagonist commits to journey (end of Act 1)

**Example** (The Hunger Games):
- Opening: Katniss hunts, cares for family
- Ordinary World: Life in District 12
- Inciting Incident: Reaping, Prim selected
- Plot Point 1: Katniss volunteers, leaves for Capitol

### Act 2: Confrontation (50%)
**Purpose**: Protagonist pursues goal, faces obstacles, learns

**Key Beats**:
1. **Rising Action**: Complications increase
2. **Midpoint**: Major reversal or revelation (50% mark)
3. **Pinch Points**: Reminders of antagonist's power
4. **All Is Lost**: Lowest point (75% mark)
5. **Second Plot Point**: Final piece of puzzle, ready for climax

**Example**:
- Rising: Training, allies, sponsors
- Midpoint: Games begin, rules change
- Pinch: Rue's death
- All Is Lost: Peeta dying, both can't win
- Plot Point 2: Berries strategy

### Act 3: Resolution (25%)
**Purpose**: Final confrontation, resolution, new normal

**Key Beats**:
1. **Climax**: Final confrontation
2. **Resolution**: Consequences play out
3. **Denouement**: New normal established

**Example**:
- Climax: Berries, both win
- Resolution: Return home
- Denouement: New fame, Capitol tension

## Alternative Structures

### The Hero's Journey
Joseph Campbell's monomyth.

**12 Stages**:
1. Ordinary World
2. Call to Adventure
3. Refusal of Call
4. Meeting the Mentor
5. Crossing Threshold
6. Tests, Allies, Enemies
7. Approach to Innermost Cave
8. Ordeal
9. Reward
10. The Road Back
11. Resurrection
12. Return with Elixir

**Used in**: Star Wars, Lord of the Rings, The Matrix

### Seven-Point Story Structure
Dan Wells' structure.

1. **Hook**: Starting point
2. **Plot Turn 1**: Call to adventure
3. **Pinch 1**: Introduction of opposition
4. **Midpoint**: Move from reaction to action
5. **Pinch 2**: Opposition wins
6. **Plot Turn 2**: Power to defeat opposition
7. **Resolution**: New situation

### Save the Cat Beat Sheet
Blake Snyder's screenplay structure (adapted for novels).

**15 Beats**:
- Opening Image
- Theme Stated
- Setup
- Catalyst
- Debate
- Break into Two
- B Story
- Fun and Games
- Midpoint
- Bad Guys Close In
- All Is Lost
- Dark Night of the Soul
- Break into Three
- Finale
- Final Image

## Scene Structure

### Scene vs. Sequel
Dwight Swain's scene/sequel pattern.

**Scene** (Action):
1. Goal: Character wants something
2. Conflict: Obstacles arise
3. Disaster: Doesn't achieve goal

**Sequel** (Reaction):
1. Reaction: Emotional response
2. Dilemma: What now?
3. Decision: Choose new goal

**Example**:
```
Scene:
- Goal: Infiltrate enemy base
- Conflict: Guards, locked doors
- Disaster: Captured

Sequel:
- Reaction: Fear, anger at failure
- Dilemma: Escape? Wait for rescue? Reveal plan?
- Decision: Fake cooperation, escape at transfer
```

## Pacing

### Fast Pacing
- Short sentences
- Action scenes
- High stakes
- Immediate danger
- Rapid dialogue

### Slow Pacing
- Longer sentences
- Description
- Reflection
- Character development
- World-building

### Rhythm
Vary pace. Intense action followed by quieter moments.

```
Fast → Slow → Fast → Slow → Fastest (climax) → Slowest (resolution)
```

## Plot vs. Story

### Story
Chronological events as they happened.

"King died. Queen died of grief."

### Plot
Events arranged for emotional impact.

"Queen died. No one knew why. Until it was discovered the King had died."

**Plot adds**:
- Causation
- Emphasis
- Suspense
- Revelation

## Subplots

### Types
- **Romantic**: Love interest complicates
- **Thematic**: Reinforces themes
- **Character**: Secondary character arc
- **Comic relief**: Tension breaker

### Integration
- Interweave with main plot
- Raise stakes when main plot lags
- Resolve before climax (usually)
- Should relate thematically

## Tension and Conflict

### External Conflict
Character vs:
- Another character
- Nature
- Society
- Technology

### Internal Conflict
Character vs:
- Self
- Beliefs
- Desires
- Past

### Best Stories Combine Both
```
External: Hero must stop villain from destroying city
Internal: Hero must overcome fear from past failure
```

## Story Questions

Plant questions to maintain reader interest.

### Types
- **Dramatic**: Will they succeed?
- **Suspense**: How will villain attack?
- **Mystery**: Who killed him?
- **Romantic**: Will they get together?

### Management
- Plant multiple questions
- Answer some, raise new ones
- Save biggest for climax

## Opening Hooks

### Start With:
- Action
- Intriguing situation
- Strong voice
- Mystery
- Change

### Don't Start With:
- Weather (unless exceptional)
- Character waking up
- Excessive description
- Info-dumping backstory

## Endings

### Satisfying Endings Must:
- Resolve main story question
- Flow logically from setup
- Feel earned, not contrived
- Match story's tone
- Close character arc

### Types
- **Closed**: All questions answered
- **Open**: Some questions remain
- **Ambiguous**: Interpretation required
- **Twist**: Surprising but inevitable in hindsight

## Common Structural Problems

### Sagging Middle
Act 2 loses momentum.

**Fix**: Midpoint twist, raise stakes, add complications.

### Deus Ex Machina
Convenient solution appears from nowhere.

**Fix**: Foreshadow solution, protagonist earns victory.

### Rushed Ending
Climax feels too fast or easy.

**Fix**: Give climax proper space, show real struggle.

### Episodic Plot
Series of unconnected events.

**Fix**: Create causation between events, escalating stakes.
